## 2. Commercial Structures Compared

### Structure A: Fixed Scope / Fixed Price
**How it works:** Defined deliverables, fixed fee, change requests priced separately.
**Pros:**
- Client knows the cost upfront → easier to approve internally
- Forces discipline in scoping → Circles does discovery before quoting
- Aligns with AU procurement norms (government + mid-market)
**Cons:**
- Scope creep is the #1 failure mode. "Vague contract language, client-initiated changes, and subjective terms" are common triggers. ([Attorney Aaron Hall](https://aaronhall.com/disputes-over-scope-creep-in-fixed-price-agreements/))
- If Circles underestimates, they eat the margin
- Doesn't align incentives with outcomes
**Best for:** Well-defined, time-boxed projects (e.g., "build this system in 12 weeks")

### Structure B: Value-Based / Outcome-Based (Performance Fee)
**How it works:** Fee tied to measurable outcomes (revenue lift, conversion improvement, capacity increase). Base fee + success bonus, or entirely performance-linked.
**Pros:**
- Aligns Circles' incentive with client's outcome → trust builder
- Can justify premium pricing (if you deliver $1M value, $250k is cheap)
- Differentiating — most agencies won't do this
**Cons:**
- Attribution disputes when "multiple factors influence results" — without clear baselines, clients may dispute charges. ([Metronome](https://metronome.com/blog/outcome-based-pricing))
- Requires a measurable lever Circles controls end-to-end
- "Inability to capture outcomes accurately can significantly impact feasibility" ([Deloitte](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/manufacturing-industrial-products/industry-4-0/xaas-outcome-based-pricing.html))
- Cash flow risk: if outcomes take 9 months, Circles waits 9 months
**Best for:** Clients with clear, measurable demand signals (waitlist → revenue, lead time → throughput)

### Structure C: Hybrid (Base + Success Fee)
**How it works:** $150k base fee (covers Circles' costs) + $100k success fee paid when agreed metric is hit (e.g., "$250k incremental revenue within 9 months").
**Pros:**
- Circles covers costs regardless → no cash flow crisis
- Client has skin in the game but also protected if outcomes are slow
- The success fee *proves* the ROI claim
- Easy to explain: "You pay $150k guaranteed. The other $100k, we only earn when you earn it back."
**Cons:**
- Adds contract complexity
- Requires iron-clad baseline agreement on day 1
- Success fee timing needs clear definition (9 months from what date?)
**Best for:** Circles' positioning. This is the model I recommend.

**Recommendation for Circles: Structure C (Hybrid).** It de-risks the client (they pay $150k instead of $250k upfront), it de-risks Circles (guaranteed base covers delivery costs), and the success fee IS the proof of ROI. The pricing model becomes the positioning.

## 3. The ROI Math

### To credibly promise "$250k return in 9 months on a $250k fee":

**Scenario: Revenue unlock for a $5M ARR company with 50% gross margin**

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Client annual revenue (run-rate) | $5,000,000 AUD |
| Gross margin | 50% |
| Monthly revenue | $416,667 |
| Incremental revenue needed to return $250k in 9 months | $250,000 |
| That = 5% revenue lift over 9 months | $250,000 |
| Or = monthly incremental revenue of $27,778 | (6.7% of monthly rev) |

**A 5% revenue lift over 9 months is achievable for a company with demonstrable unmet demand.** If they have a waitlist, the lever is conversion/capacity. If they have high churn, the lever is retention. If they have pricing headroom, the lever is AOV.

### Minimum viable client economics:
- **Run-rate revenue ≥ $3M AUD annualised** (otherwise 5% lift is only $150k — doesn't cover the fee)
- **Gross margin ≥ 40%** (need margin to absorb the fee)
- **Observable unmet demand** (waitlist, lead time, turn-aways) — this is the precondition
- **One controllable lever** (conversion, capacity, retention, or pricing) — Circles must own this
- **Baseline agreement signed day 1** — both parties agree on the starting number

## 4. AU-Specific Levers

### R&D Tax Incentive (RDTI)
The most powerful tool for reducing the effective cost of a Circles engagement:

- **For companies with turnover < $20M AUD:** Refundable tax offset of **43.5%** (18.5 percentage points above the company tax rate). ([Bulletpoint](https://www.bulletpoint.com.au/rd-tax-incentive/))
- **For companies with turnover ≥ $20M AUD:** Non-refundable offset at a rate that increases with R&D intensity.
- **Software development eligibility:** Legitimate R&D allocations for software development = 20–40%. For AI/ML = 60–90%. ([Pattens](https://pattens.com/rdti-for-software/))
- **What qualifies:** Experimental activities designed to generate new knowledge where the outcome is not known in advance. Standard website or app development does NOT qualify. But building novel systems (custom automation, AI-powered workflow tools, new platform architectures) likely DOES.

**Effective cost calculation for a $250k Circles engagement (eligible company < $20M turnover):**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Gross engagement fee | $250,000 |
| Eligible R&D proportion (conservative 30%) | $75,000 |
| RDTI rebate at 43.5% | $32,625 |
| **Net effective cost to client** | **$217,375** |

If a higher R&D proportion is defensible (say 50%): net cost = **$195,625**.
If the engagement is heavily R&D (say 70%, for a novel AI/automation build): net cost = **$163,750**.

**This is a significant de-risking tool.** Circles can legitimately say: "Your effective cost may be $165k–$217k after the R&D Tax Incentive, and we'll return that in 9 months."

**Key deadline:** 30 April 2026 registration deadline for 2024-25 activities. The "Ambitious Australia" report (March 2026) proposes the biggest restructure of the RDTI since 2011 — watch this space. ([TaxByte](https://taxbyte.com.au/research-and-development-tax-incentive-guide-2026/))

### Other Grants
- **Export Market Development Grants (EMDG):** For companies expanding into export markets. Up to 50% reimbursement on eligible export promotion expenses. Relevant if the Circles engagement helps an AU company go global (ties to R1's "ambition gap").

## 5. Documented Failures

### Failure 1: Ueno (Design Agency) — Acquired into Extinction
**What happened:** Ueno, an acclaimed Icelandic/US design agency known for premium, opinionated work, was acquired by Twitter (now X) in January 2021. Post-acquisition, the agency brand was retired, the team was absorbed, and the culture that made Ueno special was dissolved.
**What killed it:** Founder exit via acquisition. The acquirer wanted the talent, not the brand. Ueno's reputation couldn't survive without its independence.
**Lesson for Circles:** Don't build a model that depends on being acquired. The Circles brand and methodology must be the asset, not just the team.
**Source:** [PitchBook](https://pitchbook.com/profiles/company/320265-82)

### Failure 2: The Scope Creep Death Spiral (Pattern, Not Single Case)
**What happens:** Agency quotes a fixed-price project. Client requests "small changes" that accumulate. Agency absorbs them to maintain the relationship. Margin collapses. Team burns out. Quality drops. Client is unhappy despite getting more than they paid for. Both lose.
**What kills it:** "Vague contract language, client-initiated changes, and subjective terms lacking clear criteria." ([Aaron Hall](https://aaronhall.com/disputes-over-scope-creep-in-fixed-price-agreements/))
**Frequency:** One framework finds that **70% of agencies** report scope creep as a recurring problem. ([Wayfront](https://wayfront.com/blog/project-based-pricing))
**Lesson for Circles:** The hybrid model (Structure C) mitigates this: the base fee covers a defined scope, and the success fee is tied to an outcome, not a deliverable list. If the client wants more scope, it's a separate conversation.

### Failure 3: Attribution Disputes in Outcome-Based Models
**What happens:** Agency delivers work. Client's revenue goes up. Client attributes it to their own sales team, not the agency. Or: revenue goes up but client's CFO argues it was seasonal, not the agency's intervention. Agency doesn't get the success fee.
**What kills it:** "Defining how you'll attribute outcomes when multiple factors influence results is a critical challenge... customers may dispute charges, question fairness, and lose trust." ([Metronome](https://metronome.com/blog/outcome-based-pricing))
**Lesson for Circles:** The baseline agreement is everything. Both parties sign off on: (1) the metric, (2) the measurement method, (3) the attribution model, (4) the timeline. Written, signed, before work starts.

## 6. Scoping Guardrails — The Circles Qualification Test

Before quoting any engagement, Circles runs this binary test. If ANY of the first 5 are "No", walk away.

| # | Question | Pass = |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Does the company have ≥$3M AUD annualised run-rate revenue? | Yes |
| 2 | Is there an observable signal of unmet demand (waitlist, lead times, turn-aways, capacity constraint)? | Yes |
| 3 | Can we identify one lever we can control end-to-end (conversion, capacity, retention, pricing)? | Yes |
| 4 | Will the founder and an ops lead both be available for a weekly 30-min check-in for 90 days? | Yes |
| 5 | Will the client sign a baseline agreement (metric, method, attribution) on day 1? | Yes |
| 6 | Is the company eligible for the R&D Tax Incentive? (bonus — reduces effective cost) | Preferred |
| 7 | Does the founder understand that we build systems their team runs — we don't become a permanent dependency? | Yes |
